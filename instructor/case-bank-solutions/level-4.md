## PB-401 — Designing the campus lost-and-found system

**Solution guide.** Data model: ITEMS (item_id, category, found_location, photo, intake_date, status), CLAIMS (claim_id, item_id, claimant_contact, description_match, verified_date, disposition). The design's load-bearing choice is **verification by specific detail** ("what's engraved on the wallet? which keyring?") rather than identity-document matching — identity documents at intake create a privacy-holding database the grant never funded; challenge-details verify ownership *without* storing who the claimant is beyond a contact for the handover moment. Retention: one-term hold → donation, with **personal-data purge at disposition** (minimization — the record outlives the personal data). Rotability: state-machine statuses only (intake → stored → claimed/donated), photo-first records (a volunteer with any phone intakes in 90 seconds), zero free-text-required fields, one-page procedure laminated at the desk — a monthly-turnover volunteer is a design *requirement*, so the system must teach itself. Criteria table: privacy → detail-verification; accessibility → photo-first, no-jargon flow; scale (2,000/term) → state machine not prose; budget-zero → free institutional tools only; verification → challenge details + photo comparison. The knowingly under-served paragraph is the expert marker — common honest picks: photo storage scalability on zero budget (photos expire with the item), or verification strength for high-value items (escalate those to staffed hours — a named residual, not a hidden gap).
**Alternatives.** Paper-plus-spreadsheet hybrid (defensible at launch — migrate at term 2 once intake flow stabilizes); university-safety-office integration (solves liability, kills accessibility — their systems assume staff training).
**Trade-offs.** Privacy (minimal data) vs verification strength (more data = easier claims triage) — detail-challenge threads it; photo-first speed vs low-value-item noise (photos optional for category-low items, say cables).
**Common mistakes.** Full identity capture at intake (a privacy database nobody consented to); free-text descriptions as the primary key of memory (volunteers can't sustain them); a retention policy that never deletes; the under-served paragraph *omitted* (at L4, the sacrifice unstated = the constraint unrecognized).

## PB-402 — The clinic's offline-first records problem (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** The constraint *interactions* are the exam: cloud-only maximizes maintainability (one technician's dream) but fails the non-negotiable (care continues during outages) — the vendor's proposal treats availability as invisible because it's the constraint the vendor's architecture cannot serve. Local-first maximizes availability but multiplies the privacy surface (health data on local machines — theft, dust, power-cut corruption) and the technician's load (N sites × backups × patches). Hybrid caching (write locally, queue, sync on reconnect) is the honest frontier, but **its consistency story must be designed, not hoped**: low conflict rates (stated in the case) make last-writer-wins-with-audit-trail defensible; field-level merge is the upgrade if conflicts concentrate in structured fields. Recommendation: hybrid with **scope control** — offline cache only for that site's *active* patients (minimization again: the outage's blast radius is bounded by what was cached, not the whole corpus). First-outage narrative (the deliverable that proves the design): care proceeds on cached records; notes queue locally; audit logging **continues offline** (the audit requirement survives the architecture — design it in, not around); on reconnect, sync runs, conflicts (rare) surface for clinician resolution with both versions shown; the technician's morning report shows sync status per site. Technician-load criterion decides scope: one well-documented system, boring by intent.
**Alternatives.** Local-first with nightly sync (defensible if outage tolerance must be *days* — the stated pattern is hours, so hybrid suffices); paper-during-outage (the de facto status quo — formalize it as fallback, don't let it be the architecture).
**Trade-offs.** Availability (non-negotiable) vs maintainability (one technician) — hybrid spends *design* effort to avoid spending *operational* effort; consistency simplicity (LWW) vs clinical nuance (field merge) — conflict rates justify the simpler choice, say so.
**Common mistakes.** Cloud-only with "it'll mostly be online" (the non-negotiable constraint dismissed); sync-on-reconnect presented as the whole design (conflicting edits are the real test — named by the case); audit logging designed as online-only (the requirement silently dies in the outage it exists for); two clever systems instead of one boring one (technician criterion violated).

## PB-403 — The exam-grade data pipeline for 8,000 students

**Solution guide.** Pipeline stages: (1) **export contract** — a simple CSV template with a *validator at submission* (heterogeneity handled by feed-in format, not by decree: lecturers export from anything; the contract is the border); (2) **staging with provenance** — every grade traceable to file + row + rule-version (the audit spine); (3) **rules as versioned config** — the mid-flight rule change becomes *version 2 applied to a named boundary* (courses processed after timestamp T), auditable rather than retroactive chaos; (4) **exception queues** — late file → re-run affected course only; mid-pipeline correction → targeted re-run with both versions archived (nothing overwritten; superseded results remain inspectable). Rejection policy — what the pipeline must *refuse* rather than fix, with reasons: **silently-coerced types** ("pass???" as a grade must halt the row, not guess — silent fixing destroys the auditability that is the pipeline's core duty; a human resolves the row, the resolution is logged); **duplicated student IDs** (a join on duplicates silently misattributes grades — refuse and route); **cross-file aggregate mismatches** (course enrolment ≠ sum of marks → a file is stale or mis-pasted — refuse, don't reconcile silently). Privacy: per-student records flow through staging; no bulk spreadsheets emailed (L30 minimization — the pipeline *is* the alternative to email). The expert note: the design's job is to make the **honest path easier than the heroics** — validators at the border, exceptions as queues, rules as config; heroics culture dies when the structured path is faster than the workaround.
**Alternatives.** Mandated-template-with-penalties (defensible politically, fails culturally — the case says lecturers won't standardize by decree; the *validator* is the decree-proof mechanism); buy registry software (out of the scenario's scope — a student consulting team designs the pipeline; noting the boundary is fine).
**Trade-offs.** Strictness (rejections protect integrity) vs staff load (rejections create queues) — the queue design is where strictness becomes survivable; versioned rules (auditability) vs simplicity (one rulebook) — versioning costs a config discipline and buys the mid-flight change.
**Common mistakes.** Coercion-friendly ingestion (the pipeline "helpfully" fixes "pass???" — the audit trail is now fiction); rules as tribal knowledge (the mid-flight change becomes memory-dependent); full re-runs for late files (the 72-hour window dies); spreadsheets emailed between stages (the privacy leak inside the fix).

## PB-404 — Security, sustainability, and the 300-seat lab refresh

**Solution guide.** The evaluation matrix with interactions noted — the *interactions* are the exam, not the row scores: (a) replace-all — simple ops, fails embodied carbon (the carbon plan's fine print: embodied dominates, so the *best environmental machine is the one not manufactured*) and ignores the 40% idle data; (b) thin clients — minimizes embodied carbon and ops (one gold image) but **server dependency fails availability** during server incidents, and specialist seats (engineering, data science) still need real compute; (c) staggered 60% — matches spend to use, but mixed-fleet patching burden compounds and the visible inequality of new-vs-old labs is a real political cost; (d) hybrid — thin clients where general use, 20 real machines where measured need: wins the interactions but demands honest ops math. Weighted recommendation, weights shown: carbon-embodied (25%), availability/resilience (20%), cost-per-student-hour (20%), ops burden (15%), accessibility integration (10%), update-lifecycle resilience (10%) → **(d)**. Year-one security-ops plan for (d): **one gold image** (thin-client endpoint + full-PC image), scheduled patch window (L12's deliberate patching), lab network segment isolated from staff systems, incident scope = **re-image, not clean** (shared semi-public machines assume compromise — least-trust posture), adaptation seats inventoried *in* the image plan (accessibility rides on specific hardware — it must be in the image or it doesn't exist). The insight to name: the carbon plan's embodied principle *flips the default* — efficiency ratings reward running lean; the mandate rewards buying less.
**Alternatives.** (b) pure thin-client (defensible if specialist seats are genuinely few — the 20 full machines could shrink; check the utilization data's tail); (c) pure stagger (defensible if budget is truly the binding constraint — accept the patching burden explicitly).
**Trade-offs.** Embodied carbon (buy less) vs fleet simplicity (one hardware class); thin-client availability (server incidents hit everyone) vs ops simplicity (the same server is why patching is easy) — the trade is *where* risk lives, not whether it exists; visible equity (staggered refresh) vs fleet coherence.
**Common mistakes.** Efficiency-ratings-only carbon analysis (the fine print said *embodied* — the case's own hint, missed); utilization data read as "buy 60% less" (peaks exist; the data shows *when*, not only *how much* — the discussion question makes this the lesson); no interaction notes (a matrix without interactions is a spreadsheet, not an analysis); security-ops plan omitted or copied from a desktop checklist (shared-lab posture differs).

## PB-405 — The city's open-data portal (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Tiered policy: **automated publication** for structured low-risk fields (transport, budgets — open-by-default where risk is low and volume is high); **standardized-review class** for narrative-bearing data (service-request text: strip/replace free-text fields, delay publication one cycle for review — the delay is the privacy process, proportionate to two staff because it's *structural*, not per-record heroics); **paused class** only for demonstrated re-identification risk, under *published criteria* — the publication of the criteria is what stops tiers from becoming a censorship mechanism (an unpublished tier list is indistinguishable from political convenience). Privacy review, two-staff-proportionate: intake checklist + automated field-type validation + a named second reviewer for the narrative class only. Re-identification assessment method: enumerate the *linking datasets that exist* (other open data, electoral registers, published planning applications), then test the specific dataset's uniqueness on plausible joins (concept-level k-anonymity-class thinking, documented per dataset — dataset-specific, not a checkbox). Response protocol (part of the design, not an afterthought): who is notified on a discovered re-identification, the correction path, and the takedown criterion. The scandal framing, named: **the disparity analysis was the portal working** — accountability journalism is the product; the answer to it is data quality, not data suppression. Residual risk, honestly: small cells + creative linking will sometimes re-identify; the delay-and-review design reduces and never eliminates; the response protocol is what bounds the harm.
**Alternatives.** (b) open-by-default-everything (defensible on transparency-maximal grounds; the narrative-field incidents are the counter-evidence — name what changes your mind); (a) pause-pending-review (the scandal response — rejected because "pausing is easy" is the perception that kills portals).
**Trade-offs.** Transparency value (real, named) vs privacy incidents (real, named) — the tiers are where the values are *priced*; review thoroughness vs two-staff capacity — structural review (delay, automation) beats heroic review; speed of publication vs risk of small-cell re-identification.
**Common mistakes.** Tiers without published criteria (the censorship slide); per-record manual review (capacity fantasy — two staff); re-identification "assessed" generically (the risk is dataset-*specific* — method must show the joins); treating the scandal as the problem (it was the portal succeeding — the framing error that produces the wrong policy).

## PB-406 — The family's digital estate plan

**Solution guide.** Inventory method: a **classified list** — account, class (email/financial/subscriptions/photos/business), recovery mechanism, notes — stored as an encrypted note (password-manager secure note), with a paper *pointer* (not passwords) in the sealed location: the pointer says "the map is in the manager; the manager's emergency access is set up" and nothing more. Per-class mechanisms: **email** — built-in legacy/inactive-account tools (designed for exactly this; name the general mechanism, don't fabricate service specifics); **financial** — routed through each institution's documented beneficiary/estate process, **never password-sharing** (unreliable *and* policy-violating — the classic wrong answer); **subscriptions** — the executor's cancellation list (low stakes, high annoyance — worth a line each); **photos** — shared-album transfer or the legacy tool, because this is what future family actually grieves; **business accounts** — transferred via the platform's *business tools* (admin roles, page roles — designed for ownership transfer, unlike personal profiles). Plan security: the pointer reveals nothing without the manager's emergency access, which requires the service's own dead-man verification — the plan is not itself a breach vector. Boundaries: **no jurisdiction-specific legal claims** (wills, inheritance law — flag for a professional, don't improvise); no circumvention of service terms; no assuming today's mechanisms persist — **annual review is part of the design** (a stale plan is an untested backup, PB-213 at decade scale). The dignity thread: drafted *with* the relatives — their wishes recorded, not imposed.
**Alternatives.** Everything-on-paper-in-the-safe (the classic wrong answer — a breach vector that also rots; say *why* it fails, not just that it does); commercial estate-service platforms (defensible if the family will maintain it — same annual-review discipline applies).
**Trade-offs.** Access (the executor's needs) vs security (the plan as attack surface) — the pointer/emergency-access split threads it; completeness vs maintainability — classes, not every account.
**Common mistakes.** Paper password list (both unreliable and a liability); legal improvisation (jurisdiction-dependent territory — the case says flag it); one-time plan with no review cycle (decay is certain); inventory as "every account ever" (unsustainable — classes serve the executor; stragglers are what legacy tools handle).

## PB-407 — The lecture-capture system with a maintenance cliff

**Solution guide.** Criteria table with the two rows weak answers miss — **caption debt** (a compounding liability: manual captioning backlogs grow faster than they shrink; machine transcription + human correction is the only trajectory where the debt *shrinks*) and **true cost** (subscription fee vs admin-hours × salary, *shown* not asserted — self-hosted "free" labour is the classic hidden line). Criteria: continuity risk, true cost, caption trajectory, residency compliance, single-admin sustainability. The **verification step precedes the decision**: residency policy requires regional hosting; the cloud product *offers* a region — verify it contractually before choosing (a) — this ordering is the expert discipline (verify the binding constraint before falling in love with an option). Decision commonly: (a) if residency verifies; (b) if it doesn't — the decision flips on a checkable fact, which is why the check comes first. Migration plan, semester-boundary aware: phase 1 — dual-run one course on the new system (learn the workflow at low stakes); phase 2 — bulk archive transfer (400 hours) + redirect links (old URLs keep working); phase 3 — in-flight semester cuts over at the boundary, never mid-semester. Tripwire: "if the vendor's regional hosting lapses, or caption correction quality degrades below [named threshold], the decision re-opens" — the reversal condition is what makes the choice a decision rather than a bet.
**Alternatives.** (c) minimal pivot (defensible on cost; the room-automation integration loss is a per-lecture friction tax — price it honestly if proposing); do-nothing-until-forced (the cliff is dated — sunsetting vendors stop patching before they stop selling).
**Trade-offs.** Subscription visibility vs self-host labour invisibility (the true-cost row is where the trade lives); caption automation (debt shrinks) vs correction quality (machine errors need human passes — the workflow must exist, not just the feature); vendor dependence vs one-admin reality (dependence is *bought* because the alternative exceeds the admin's capacity — honest, not happy).
**Common mistakes.** Licence-fee comparisons only (the hidden labour line decides the case and goes unexamined); captions as operational detail (they're a *decision input* — the debt compounds in every option, unequally); residency assumed rather than verified (the decision rests on an unchecked fact); migration mid-semester (the continuity constraint named by the case, ignored).

## PB-408 — The neighborhood mesh network proposal (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Architecture: **distributed individual shares over a collective exit node** — each host shares their own subscription under their own name, mesh only for genuine dead zones. Why: abuse responsibility defaults to the subscription holder; under a collective exit, one anonymous abuser's traffic makes *the whole neighbourhood* the liable party — the collective node concentrates the proposal's fatal risk (trace the scenario: abuse → report → the exit-node holder has no attribution → the ISP acts against the holder → trust collapses cluster-wide). Governance: a named small committee, simple written rules (who may host, what happens on an abuse report: suspend → investigate → document), and a **written wind-down plan** — the mesh's death should be a *planned event*, not a decay (PB-312's law at infrastructure scale). Security posture: guest-network isolation per host (host family's devices never share the guest VLAN — no transit of family traffic), exit-node abuse protocol, user education at onboarding (what guests may do, what hosts may see). Sustainability: hardware lifecycle fund (small monthly seed — routers die outdoors in 3–5 years), spares pool, **volunteer pairing** (no single points of human failure). Mission paragraph: preserves affordable access where feasible; **gives up** "free for all" (hosted shares have limits) and "no rules" (rules are what make it last); honest capacity note — mesh hops throttle backhaul, so dead zones get *coverage, not speed*.
**Alternatives.** Collective exit with strict logging (defensible only with privacy-destroying attribution — the logging defeats the neighbours' own privacy; name that cost); advocate-for-cheap-ISP instead (out of the group's control — the redesign is what they *can* do).
**Trade-offs.** Coverage ambition (the mesh dream) vs liability concentration (the collective node); host generosity (their subscription shared) vs their exposure (their name on the account); volunteer enthusiasm vs succession design.
**Common mistakes.** Keeping the collective exit node (the fatal concentration — the case's core test); no abuse protocol (the first incident ends the project); "volunteers will maintain it" (burnout is a *design input*, not a hope); wind-down never written (the project becomes a slow-moving liability when enthusiasm fades).

## PB-409 — The AI TA at exam scale (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Boundary design, three classes: **course-material-grounded answers with citations** (the vendor supports scoped grounding — hallucination class 1 addressed at the architecture level); **deterministic FAQ layer for exam/assessment rules** — policy questions *never* reach the generative model (the pilot's exam-rule incident defines class 2: rare, high-stakes, perfectly suited to a curated, versioned FAQ the TA cites verbatim); **graceful refusal outside scope** with human-redirect (class 3 — the refusal *is* a feature; it defines where office hours matter). Monitoring: sampled answer audits weekly + incident reports + question-class logs — the three pilot incident classes each get a tripwire (e.g., "any exam-rule question answered generatively = incident"). Equity mechanisms: use-data reviewed per cohort **by access pattern** (not demographics — aggregate privacy-respecting); alternative paths kept *equivalent* (the FAQ layer is published to everyone, not only inside the TA — non-users get the same answers); disclosure that normalizes both use and non-use. Human floor: office hours remain for **ambiguity, appeals, and the refusal class** — the floor's argument is the refusal class (a generative system's boundary is exactly where human judgement is irreplaceable), not sentimentality. Non-transferable pilot evidence, named: satisfaction and time-saved came from a **self-selected cohort of 80 enthusiasts with small-N incidents** — neither predicts 1,200-user behaviour; the 15× scale changes error propagation (one exam-rule error × 1,200 = a cohort-wide misunderstanding), not just volume.
**Alternatives.** No AI TA (defensible if office hours genuinely cover demand — but the pilot's 4 hours/week saved suggests they don't); TA-with-full-scope-plus-monitoring (defensible only with the monitoring resourced before launch — the sequencing is the ethics).
**Trade-offs.** Coverage (24/7 answers) vs accuracy accountability (the FAQ layer's rigidity) — rigidity where stakes are high is the point; scale economics (the dean's interest) vs guardrail cost (monitoring is labour — budget it, don't assume it).
**Common mistakes.** Scaling on pilot satisfaction (the self-selection blindspot — name it or repeat it); generative answers to policy questions (the one class with a deterministic cure, left generative); equity as a demographic afterthought (access-pattern review is the design, not a report); office hours cut because "the TA covers it" (the illusion of coverage — the case's named trap).

## PB-410 — The four-year phone-lab sustainability project

**Solution guide.** Evaluation matrix with tensions: (a) BYOD-minimum-spec — cheapest carbon (no new devices) but **fails equity as a hard constraint** (a student must never be unable to participate — the case states it as absolute); (b) 80 new standard units — worst embodied carbon *and* creates the exact waste the policy targets (functional devices retired on a refresh clock); (c) BYOD + new loaner pool — fixes equity but repeats (b)'s carbon in miniature; (d) **BYOD + refurbished pool** — equity served, near-zero new embodied carbon, the refurbishment sector strengthened, end-of-life *easier* (already once-cycled devices). Weighted recommendation with weights shown → (d). The **purchase filter that makes (d) survive the security constraint**: devices enter the pool only with **≥3 years of OS updates remaining** — support horizon is a procurement criterion, checked at intake, not discovered at year two (PB-407's cliff, dodged at the door). End-of-life path, per device class: teaching life (4y) → loaner downgrade (1–2y further) → accredited refurbisher/reseller (second life) → certified recycler only at true end — **data-wipe certificate at every transition** (the security constraint persists past use). Heterogeneity: minimum-spec + emulator for edge cases rather than fleet standardization (the spec doc is the chaos-preventer; the emulator catches the stragglers).
**Alternatives.** (b) with honest carbon accounting (defensible if teaching consistency outweighs carbon — argue it explicitly, don't drift into it); (c) with refurbished-later pool (nearly (d) — distinguish by *when* the pool is built).
**Trade-offs.** Equity (hard constraint) vs carbon (mandate) — the pool is how both survive, but the pool's *provenance* (refurbished vs new) is where the carbon is decided; fleet consistency (teaching simplicity) vs device diversity (real-world honesty) — minimum-spec threads it.
**Common mistakes.** Equity via exceptions ("see me if you can't get one" — stigma + unreliability; the pool is *architecture*, not charity); refresh-clock thinking (retiring functional devices is the named waste — the lifecycle path is the answer); update horizons ignored at intake (the cliff arrives mid-course); end-of-life as an afterthought ("we'll figure it out in year four" — the policy targets exactly that figure-it-out).

## PB-411 — The hospital's alert-fatigue problem (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Mechanism: alert fatigue is a **signal-to-noise problem with human cost on both sides** — 200+ alerts/shift with 85–90% minor-or-false trains desensitization; sensitivity to *rare* critical signals decays with noise exposure (cry-wolf dynamics, formalized); the system's failure is not volume but the noise-to-consequence ratio. The AI layer's risk profile: suppression optimizes **average workload**, but its errors concentrate where the human is *already* desensitized — a suppressed true-critical meets an inattentive nurse: **errors compound, not add** (suppression-error × desensitization is multiplicative; that's the mechanism the discussion question asks for). Also: priority labels come from *the same noisy system* — the AI inherits the misclassification that caused the near-miss. Investigation design for the near-miss: **systemic, not blame** — trace the misclassification source (which system labelled it low priority, with what data), review the reporting culture of the unit (do nurses report near-misses, or hide them?), and treat the near-miss as the system's *free sample* of its own failure mode. Implementation path: **shadow mode first** — the AI classifies but suppresses nothing; human disagreement is logged as the training/validation dataset; then narrow-class suppression with **hard floors** (certain alert classes — cardiac-class, airway-class — never suppressible, by rule, not by model confidence). Monitoring: weekly disagreement audits; **suppressed-alert outcome tracing** (every suppressed alert whose patient later deteriorates gets review — the tripwire that catches a suppressed-alert disaster before it compounds); a standing nurse escalation path requiring no justification (reporting friction is how the last near-miss stayed silent).
**Alternatives.** No AI layer, fix thresholds manually (defensible if the misclassification source is fixable — the audit may reveal the cheap fix); add staff instead (attacks workload, not signal-to-noise; costs more than anyone has).
**Trade-offs.** Average workload (the vendor's number) vs tail risk (the suppressed-alert catastrophe — the nursing lead's fear, formalized); suppression benefits (now) vs desensitization feedback (later) — shadow mode and outcome tracing are the bridges.
**Common mistakes.** Treating fatigue as a volume problem ("fewer alerts = better" without the signal-to-noise mechanism); average-based evaluation of the vendor claim (60% reduction of *what class*? the tail is where safety lives); blame-first investigation (the reporting culture dies, and with it the tripwires); suppression without hard floors (the model's confidence is not a safety argument).

## PB-412 — The archive of a closed social platform

**Solution guide.** Consent framework: closure changes consent even with no post's content changing — **context collapse**: posts made to a niche community under one privacy regime become *permanently public artefacts* in a new context; retroactive perfect consent is unattainable, so the design centers the **right to object**: in-app notification + opt-out window (posts removed on request), and the archive ships with a published context statement ("preserved because the platform closed; not for new distribution") — the statement is what keeps the consent story honest. Scope policy, criteria-based: full text + images by default; embedded third-party media by **link inventory only** (dead links *documented* — honest link-rot accounting rather than pretending they'll resolve); private/deleted content excluded absolutely (the platform's own deletion was an act — respect it); the 90-day window bounds ambition — the criteria decide what's cut, not panic. Technical: export (400 GB, undocumented schema) → **schema inference documented as inferred, never authoritative** → conversion to open formats (plain text, standard images) → static browsable archive + **checksummed preservation masters**. Handover (PB-312 at corpus scale): three institutional copies (3-2-1), a named steward organization, **annual fixity check ritual**, a README in the PB-316 tradition — the archive's context is its survival. Knowingly lost, each with its criterion: dynamic features (search-as-was, threaded notifications — the static conversion's price); dead embedded media (link-inventory criterion); opted-out posts (the consent principle outranks completeness — say that ordering explicitly).
**Alternatives.** Full-fidelity database preservation (defensible if a future platform migration is funded — but that's a bet against the 90-day volunteer reality; the static archive is the deliverable that *ships*); wait-for-a-buyer (the closure is announced — waiting is the risk).
**Trade-offs.** Consent (opt-out window) vs completeness (removed posts leave gaps) — consent wins, named; fidelity (dynamic features) vs deliverability (static archive ships in 90 days); curation labour vs scope — criteria are the rationing device.
**Common mistakes.** Preserving deleted/private content "for completeness" (the absolute exclusion exists because the platform's deletion was an act of *its users*); schema inference presented as authoritative (future researchers deserve to know it was inferred); no fixity ritual (bit-rot is silent — the annual check is the archive's pulse); consent as a single blanket notice (the right to *object individually* is the mechanism that matters).

## PB-413 — The exam-portal rewrite under a live semester

**Solution guide.** Approach: **(b) strangler migration** — big-bang (a) bets the firm on a single cutover with unproven reliability at exam scale; the old system's exam-window behaviour is *battle-tested* — a rewrite must **earn** that reliability, not assume it; hardening-first (c) is defensible if runway were longer, but defers the structural problems clients already feel. Knowledge-capture plan (deadline-bound, because the developer exits in six weeks): the departing developer writes the **operations document** as their final deliverable — deployment procedure, failure modes, the exam-window runbook, and the undocumented quirks ("the Tuesday deploy rule" class of knowledge: things everyone does and nobody wrote down) — pair-documented with the new team **while they can still ask**, reviewed by the new team before the exit date. The deadline changes the content: a doc written *for handover* records *why* and *what breaks*; a doc written for markers records *what it is*. Transition security posture: the old system's patching **accelerates** during migration (it runs longer — the transition must not become a security holiday); auth migration early (credentials handled once, carefully — not scattered across the migration); exam-window monitoring unchanged. Cutover safety net: **per-feature flags** (instant rollback per feature, not per system), **parallel-run verification for grading** (both systems compute, compare, alert on divergence), the **exam-window freeze ritual** (no migrations inside exam windows — scheduled around the client calendar). Firm-killing failure mode, named: **the first exam window on the new booking path failing at load** — mitigation: load-test at 3× last year's peak, rehearsed feature-flag rollback, and the **old path kept warm** (not deleted) for two full exam cycles.
**Alternatives.** (a) big-bang (defensible only if the codebase is too small for incremental routing — 30 clients and 200k takes say otherwise); (c) hardening-first (defensible if clients' feature demands were soft — the case says they're not).
**Trade-offs.** Migration duration (strangler runs two systems for months — cost and cognitive load) vs cutover risk (big-bang's all-or-nothing); old-system patching (accelerated spend on a system being replaced) vs the security holiday (the alternative); client feature pressure vs the freeze ritual (features wait; trust doesn't recover).
**Common mistakes.** Rewriting for reliability (the old system's reliability is an *asset to match*, the case's own hint); knowledge capture as "write docs when you can" (the deadline is what makes the doc *different* — the exit date is the design input); feature-flag-less cutover (rollback at system granularity is not rollback); deleting the old path after cutover week (the first exam window deserves a warm fallback).

## PB-414 — The two-town broadband decision (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Criteria framework: unserved-households connected per unit subsidy (the mandate's *own* metric — use it), deliverability risk, trust-adjusted adoption (B's collapsed initiative discounts willingness to pre-pay), sustainability cost, precedent value. **Trust as an engineering constraint**: B's trust damage shapes the *design*, not just the risk score — a B intervention that requires residents to pre-pay anything repeats the collapse's wound; therefore B's design is grant-funded build, **public accounts, elected oversight** — the governance reforms the collapse implies, embedded in the architecture. Per-town design (different technologies are legitimate): **A = subsidized fixed-line via ISP co-investment** (density does the work; subsidy per household is low; the ISPs' willingness is the co-investment case); **B = community-owned fixed-wireless** (tower-based suits scattered geography at 3–5× lower per-household cost than fixed-line). Sustainability: tower power (solar + grid mix), maintenance funded by a **ring-fenced operations line** (not capital budget — the collapsed initiative died of operations starvation, and that's a design lesson, not an anecdote), hardware lifecycle plan. Political-economy answer, three elements: **transparency** (both towns see the same criteria math — no backroom allocation); **sequencing commitment** (B in year 1 — *because* its unserved rate is higher, the mandate says so; A's co-investment timeline is independent); **the commitment instrument** (B's year-2 tranche contractually reserved — the promise survives election cycles). **Alternatives.** Both-towns-minimal (defensible on equity optics — rejected on deliverability: two half-builds risk two disappointments; say why); A-first-for-revenue (the economics answer — rejected because the mandate's metric is unserved households, not connections).
**Trade-offs.** Equity (B first) vs economics (A's returns) — the mandate's own metric resolves it, but the *announcement* must survive the losing town; quality difference (named) vs access floor (guaranteed); trust repair (governance reforms cost control) vs speed (reforms take a season).
**Common mistakes.** Treating B's trust damage as a risk score (it's a *design constraint* — the difference between discounting B and redesigning for B); operations funded from capital (the collapse repeats in year three); no commitment instrument (the year-2 promise dies with the election); hiding the quality difference (it surfaces anyway — owned, it's a floor; hidden, it's a betrayal).

## PB-415 — The museum's interactive exhibit for every visitor

## PB-416 — The university's AI-policy design (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Diagnosis, mechanism each: (1) **"AI-generated" has no defensible boundary** (grammar tools, autocomplete, code assistants — the definition problem is structural, not drafting); (2) **detection errors punish asymmetrically** (documented false-positive rates skew across language backgrounds — an equity failure that also produces the misfired-accusation risk); (3) **an unenforceable rule with 8% compliance teaches rule-contempt** — the integrity harm the policy meant to prevent, manufactured by the policy itself. Architecture: **university level sets principles** (honest process, no misrepresentation, evidence standards) and **course level sets rules** — per-assessment AI stance from a defined menu (required / permitted-with-disclosure / restricted / barred), each stance with its evidence expectations. The counter-intuitive move, defended: course-level rules *strengthen* integrity because enforceable local rules rebuild the credibility the unenforceable global one spent — diversity is accommodated by design, not flattened. Enforcement: suspicion triggers **process-evidence review** (drafts, version history, structured oral check) rather than detector scores; detector output may **prompt** review, never **constitute** proof (the error-asymmetry default). Review cycle: annual, reviewing disclosure rates and accusation-appeal data, revising the menu — the policy is versioned like the code in PB-413. Cannot-achieve paragraph: the policy cannot prevent every misuse, cannot define the boundary perfectly, and **cannot substitute for assessment design that makes AI use visible** — redesigning assessment is the long-term answer the policy can only nudge.
**Alternatives.** Keep-the-ban-plus-training (defensible as political sequencing — but the 8% rate says credibility is already spent; retraining doesn't re-fund it); detection-tool mandate with appeal rights (the appeals become the policy — false-positive volume is the case's own evidence).
**Trade-offs.** Uniformity (one rule, unenforceable) vs diversity (many rules, enforceable) — the menu spends consistency to buy credibility; detection convenience (cheap flags) vs due process (process-evidence review costs staff time) — the asymmetry of error prices the trade.
**Common mistakes.** Redefining "AI-generated" harder (the boundary problem is structural — effort spent re-defining is effort spent failing); detector scores as evidence (the error-asymmetry veto); university-level rules only (the departments' diversity is *reality* — a policy that ignores it governs nothing); no review cycle (the two-semesters-of-evidence pattern repeats, versioned worse).

## PB-418 — The code club's device fleet across four years

**Solution guide.** Acceptance policy: a **published spec** (device classes accepted, minimum specs, what's declined — CRTs, broken screens, anything pre-dating the standard image) with a **graceful-refusal script** (donations that burden volunteers are net-negative — refused *with reasons and alternatives*, e.g., "we can't use this, but [refurbisher] takes working laptops"). Imaging/maintenance: **one standard image per device class** (browser-based curriculum, local child accounts, network isolation at the router — child-safety non-negotiables baked into the image), and the **"re-image, don't repair"** rule — thin-skill compatible because the image *is* the repair: a machine misbehaves → re-image in 20 minutes → teaching continues; per-device debugging is the skill the club doesn't have and the rule removes the need. **Trade-offs.** Donation goodwill (saying yes feels kind) vs volunteer capacity (yes costs hours forever) — the spec is how you say no kindly; hardware revival (fixing faults) vs re-image simplicity (the rule's trade, named); fleet size (every child a machine) vs spares buffer (the loaner pool is what stops a dead machine stopping a lesson).
**Common mistakes.** Accept-everything (the burden arrives weekly); repair culture (depends on the expert who leaves); breakage-based retirement (machines teaching on unpatched OSes — the security constraint violated precisely because the machine "still works"); no wipe certification (data sanitation is legal *and* ethical — the certificate is the proof the chain exists).

## PB-419 — The open-data research replication crisis (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Provenance audit, three mechanical failures: **silent upstream revision** (no snapshot pinning — the download scripts hit data that changed mid-project; the scripts are deterministic, *the inputs weren't*); **identifier re-use** (joins assumed stable district codes — ~3% of rows re-used codes, silently merging different districts); **unresolvable README** (documentation pointed at a *portal*, not an *artifact* — "data from govt portal, March" describes nothing reproducible once the portal revises). Re-analysis protocol: **freeze artifacts** (third-party archived snapshots + current portal data as *separate named datasets*), run the pipeline on both, report the delta — if the headline claim survives the frozen-data version, say so *with the revision-sensitivity quantified*; if it doesn't, the claim retreats and the erratum says which comparison is unsupported. Erratum strategy: **calibrated language** ("the district-level comparison is sensitive to the 2019 definition revision; the directional finding holds under snapshot X but not snapshot Y") — no theatrical self-flagellation, no blame toward the junior researcher (the systemic frame — no provenance tooling existed — is also the *true* frame), and **no reverse-conclusion** (absence of support ≠ evidence of the opposite). Pipeline redesign, immune-by-construction: **snapshot-pinned ingestion** (every download archived with hash — the pin is the provenance), **identifier validation gates** (reject joins on re-used codes rather than merging silently — PB-403's rejection policy, at research scale), a README that **names artifacts by hash** — reproducibility as engineering, not documentation aspiration. Must-not paragraph: must not re-run until inputs are pinned (re-running on drifting data manufactures false precision); must not scapegoat the junior researcher; must not let erratum caution become an opposite claim.
**Alternatives.** Withdraw-the-paper (defensible if *no* comparison survives freezing — the protocol is what discovers that); blame-the-portal (the publisher's revision practice is a *finding* to report, not a defence — both things are true and the response says both).
**Trade-offs.** Transparency (the full mechanical audit, published) vs the group's reputation (the audit is unflattering) — transparency is the only recovery path *and* the field's need; speed of response (the journal is waiting) vs pinning-first discipline (the re-run must wait for the freeze) — the sequencing is the integrity.
**Common mistakes.** Re-running immediately ("the data's still there" — it isn't, *the same data* isn't, and that's the finding); determinism as defence ("our scripts are fine" — the scripts were never the problem); scapegoating (the junior researcher becomes the error message — the systemic frame prevents the next crisis, the blame frame just ends careers); erratum overshoot ("the paper is wrong" when the finding is "one comparison is unsupported" — calibration has a floor and a ceiling).

## PB-421 — The whistleblower's data dilemma (conceptual)

**Solution guide.** Guidance shape: **options with consequences, never the decision** — "here are the paths; you choose; here's what each costs" is the friend's whole job; deciding *for* the officer exceeds the role and binds the friend to an outcome they can't control. Integrity analysis, precise about what's established: **hashing the files + a dated written account now** establishes *consistency over time* — the evidence's content today can be compared against itself later; it does **not** prove authenticity or origin (a hash proves the file didn't change *since hashing*, nothing about before) — the gap between *consistent* and *authentic* is the crux of the advice, and saying so plainly is the honesty the case grades. Territory map: **lawyer-now** (the unauthorized copying's legal exposure — even sympathetic copying of your own duties' records has policy/legal weight; jurisdiction's whistleblower protections; employment risk); **board-policy-now** (the reporting duty — the NGO's own policy requires board reporting, and using it is both required *and protective*: the policy channel **timestamps the disclosure**); **friend-discussable** (storage hygiene, documentation habits, emotional load — the human layer). **Trade-offs.** Loyalty (helping a friend act) vs role limits (the friend's competence boundary) — the boundary statement *is* the loyalty, expressed honestly; speed (evidence decays, memories fade) vs legality (the copying question needs professional advice before any storage decision) — the dated account and hashing are the speed-safe middle; protection (the officer's) vs due process (the manager's) — both survive only through channel-and-document discipline.
**Common mistakes.** Deciding for the officer (the friendship becomes a co-conspirator shape); hashing presented as proving authenticity (the consistency/authenticity gap — the case's central precision test); bypassing the policy channel (it feels protective, it's the opposite — the timestamp *is* the protection); spreading allegations to "build support" (surrenders protections, harms due process, and makes the officer the story).

## PB-425 — The capstone: design the course's own problem bank

**Solution guide.** Contribution spec: the existing template **as the submission format**, validator-enforced (students learn metadata discipline by using it — the validator is the teacher); the **"solvable-and-grounded" test** as the heart of the spec: a case is acceptable iff a competent peer can produce a defensible answer *from the stated information alone* — no insider knowledge required (grounded) — and constraints must interact (solvable ≠ trivial: a case with one obvious answer is a quiz, not a case). **Alternatives.** Instructor-only curation (defensible for year one's pipeline — but abandons the pedagogical inversion, which is the design's point); fully-open contribution without curation (rejected: quality collapse in one term — the triage rubric is the spec's spine).
**Trade-offs.** Openness (any student can contribute) vs quality (the rubric's gate) — the revision loop keeps both; authorship credit (retained) vs sanitization control (curators may edit) — the credit survives the edit, the control survives the credit; pedagogical ambition (the mini-unit costs a week of syllabus) vs bank quality (unscaffolded contributions flood the triage queue) — the scaffold *is* the labour-saving device.
**Common mistakes.** Honor-system consent (a checklist step, not a promise — structural, or it fails silently); leak-prevention by policy ("solutions live elsewhere" — the validator exists because policies leak); no resourced curation (the TA-plus-enthusiasm model burns out by year two — the named failure mode); scaffolding skipped ("students will figure out case design" — they'll produce quizzes with insider answers, and the triage queue drowns).
