## Why this course exists

Computers are everywhere, yet most people use them as a mystery box. This course opens the box. Starting from zero background, you will build a working mental model of computing — what happens inside the machine when you type, click, stream, and search — and then put that model to work: creating documents and spreadsheets that behave, protecting your accounts and privacy, organizing data so it can be analyzed, and evaluating the claims made about AI and other emerging technologies.

The skills are foundational for both host programs. **Computer Science** students get the conceptual vocabulary (architecture, data representation, logic, networks, databases) that later programming and systems courses assume. **Data Science** students get the computing substrate (how data is stored, encoded, moved, and queried) that later analytics courses build on. And because every concept is taught through realistic problems — not just definitions — the course doubles as structured practice in careful, step-by-step thinking.

No prior computing study is required. Weekly hands-on labs and a semester-long project turn concepts into skills.

## What you will be able to do (course learning outcomes)

On successful completion of the course you will be able to:

1. **Describe** the historical evolution of computing and classify types of computers by capability and application domain.
2. **Explain** the von Neumann model and the function of CPU, memory hierarchy, storage, and peripherals.
3. **Distinguish** system from application software and demonstrate operating-system and file-management operations.
4. **Convert** numbers among binary, octal, decimal, and hexadecimal and explain how text, images, audio, and video are encoded.
5. **Construct** truth tables and predict the behaviour of simple combinational circuits built from AND, OR, NOT, XOR, NAND, NOR.
6. **Use** word-processing, spreadsheet, and presentation tools, including formulas and charts, to complete realistic digital workflows.
7. **Explain** networking, internet, and web fundamentals (protocols, IP/DNS, HTTP) and the cloud service models IaaS/PaaS/SaaS.
8. **Explain** relational database concepts (tables, keys, queries, integrity) and demonstrate elementary queries.
9. **Apply** computational thinking — decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithm design — to small problems.
10. **Identify** common security and privacy threats and apply safe, legal, ethical practices, including critical evaluation of AI-generated output.
11. **Synthesize** course concepts in an integrated semester project (design document + working artefact + presentation).

The full official list, with policies and assessment scheme, is on the [Syllabus page](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/syllabus.html).

## Problem-solving case studies

Learning to *solve* problems — not just recite facts — is the course's through-line. The case bank gives you structured practice:

- **104 cases total**, all built on fictional but realistic scenarios.
  - **4 flagship case studies** woven into the lectures (linked in the course-contents tables above).
  - **100 banked cases** in four difficulty levels: **25 beginner** (define the real problem and its stakeholders), **25 intermediate** (decompose and compare options, with real numbers), **25 advanced** (justify a choice against explicit criteria), and **25 expert** (balance interacting constraints — budget, security, accessibility, sustainability — and defend the trade-offs).
- **Domains** span hardware buying and troubleshooting, file management and backups, operating systems, productivity, data representation, networks and the web, security and privacy, digital citizenship, databases, cloud, AI literacy, data-science workflows, university administration, healthcare information systems (conceptual), small business, education, public services, accessibility, sustainability, and ethics.
- **How to use them:** read the scenario, state the problem in your own words, list constraints and stakeholders *before* proposing anything, then compare at least two defensible options and name what each sacrifices. Model solutions are discussed in class — the point is your reasoning, not matching an answer.
- **Start here:** the [case-studies index](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/case-studies/index.html) for the flagship cases, and the [case-bank index](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/case-bank/index.html) for the graded bank, difficulty guide, and classroom method.

## The course learning path

Work through the course in this order — every stage assumes the ones before it:

1. **Computer foundations** — what a computer is, where it came from, and its place in society (L01–L04).
2. **Hardware and software** — inside the machine; how programs run; operating systems and files (L05–L12).
3. **Data representation** — binary, hexadecimal, characters, images, sound, and compression (L13–L16). *Midterm checkpoint after L16.*
4. **Logic and productivity** — Boolean logic and gates, then professional documents, spreadsheets, and presentations (L17–L20).
5. **Networking, internet, and cloud** — how data moves; what actually happens when you open a web page; cloud service models (L21–L24).
6. **Security and digital citizenship** — threats, defences, privacy, and ethical participation online (L29–L30 concepts thread throughout, concentrated here).
7. **Databases, AI, and data-science foundations** — organizing data, querying it, and evaluating intelligent systems responsibly (L25–L28).
8. **Problem-solving and integration** — computational thinking, emerging technologies, and the project showcase (L31–L32).

The [learning pathway page](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/pathway.html) turns this into a week-by-week route with checkpoints and catch-up advice if you fall behind.

## How to use this course

**If you are a student:**

1. Start at the [course home](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/) and read the [syllabus](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/syllabus.html) — especially the assessment scheme and policies.
2. Follow the [calendar](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/schedule-calendar.html) each week: read the lecture page *before* class, attempt its *Check your understanding* questions after.
3. Complete each lab in its submission window (deadlines are on the calendar); labs are graded against published rubric criteria.
4. Practice with the matching quiz and a case study at your level each week — the [pathway](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/pathway.html) shows which.
5. Stuck? The [Help page](https://nadeem-majeedch.github.io/Introduction-to-Computer-ICT-Course/help.html) lists study strategies and support routes. Ask early — concepts build on each other.

**If you are an instructor:** the website carries all student-facing materials; a complete instructor package (teaching guides, slide decks, speaker notes, exam banks with marking schemes, case-solution guides) accompanies this repository but is kept out of the published site by automated guardrails. See the maintainers section below.

**If you are a self-learner:** skip the graded deadlines and follow the [learning path](#the-course-learning-path) at your own pace — every lecture page, quiz, and case is self-contained.
