# Problem-Solving Case Bank — Index and Instructor Guide (public overview)

**100 cases** · four progressive levels · 23 domains · linked across all 32 lectures.

This page is the public entry point to the case bank. It explains how the bank is organized, how cases are used in class, and how they are assessed. Instructor-only solution guides for every case live outside the published site (see *Content protection* below).

---

## 1. The bank at a glance

| Level | Cases | Focus | What a student must now do |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Level 1 — Beginner](level-1.md) | PB-101–125 | Understand, identify, define | Locate the real problem, name stakeholders, state inputs/outputs/constraints |
| [Level 2 — Intermediate](level-2.md) | PB-201–225 | Decompose, compare | Break a problem into parts, quantify at least one comparison, name a trade-off |
| [Level 3 — Advanced](level-3.md) | PB-301–325 | Criteria-first justification | Build an explicit criteria set, score options, defend a defensible position against counter-arguments |
| [Level 4 — Expert](level-4.md) | PB-401–425 | Multi-constraint design | Design under interacting constraints (budget, security, privacy, accessibility, sustainability…), state what is knowingly under-served |

**Progression spine.** Each level assumes the previous one's skill *and adds a new move*:

1. **L1 → L2:** from *identifying* the problem to *decomposing* it and *comparing* candidate solutions.
2. **L2 → L3:** from comparisons to *explicit criteria* — weights, scoring tables, and defences against the strongest counter-argument.
3. **L3 → L4:** from single-track decisions to *interacting constraints*, where every choice helps one stakeholder and costs another, and the best answers name what they sacrificed.

Cases are deliberately **not programming exercises**. They span conceptual, practical, organizational, ethical, and technical ICT problems — see the domain matrix in §4.

## 2. Every case, one template

All 100 cases share the same structure (enforced by the bank validator):

1. **Unique case ID** (`PB-LvlNN`) and **difficulty level**
2. **Title** and **domain**
3. **Related lecture(s)** — linked to the course's lecture pages
4. **Scenario** — realistic, fictional or generalized
5. **Problem statement** — the actual ask
6. **Stakeholders** · **Learning objectives** · **Constraints**
7. **Available information** — what a solver may legitimately use
8. **Student task** · **Suggested thinking time**
9. **Expected solution characteristics** *(public — describes the *shape* of good reasoning, never the answer)*
10. **Discussion questions** · **Extension challenge** · **References**

The instructor-only companion (fields 15–18 of the full teaching template: solution guide, alternative solutions, trade-off analysis, common mistakes) is kept outside the published website entirely — see §6.

> **Design note.** *Expected solution characteristics* is public on purpose: it tells students what *good reasoning looks like* (decomposition, criteria, named trade-offs) without handing them a conclusion. Cases like PB-215, PB-314, and PB-417 have genuinely multiple defensible answers, and their characteristics sections say so.

## 3. Classroom method

The recommended pattern (adapt freely — cases differ in length and stakes; do **not** force every case into an identical timing block):

1. **Display the case** (or distribute it). Read-through only — no analysis yet.
2. **Independent analysis** for the suggested thinking time. Each student drafts: the real problem, stakeholders, constraints, and a first-cut direction.
3. **Group discussion** (pairs or fours). Compare problem definitions *before* solutions — most disagreements trace to definitional differences, and surfacing them is the lesson.
4. **Proposals** — each group states a solution direction in two minutes.
5. **Compare approaches** across groups: where do they agree, where do criteria (not conclusions) differ?
6. **Reveal or discuss the instructor solution guide** — focus on its *reasoning pattern*, not its verdict.
7. **Trade-offs and limitations** — what did the guide's approach cost? Whose interests did it deprioritize?
8. **Extension question** — the case's extension challenge, or a twist ("the budget just halved — now what?").

**Timing guidance.**

| Case length | Independent | Group | Compare + reveal | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short (L1) | 8–10 min | 10 min | 10 min | ~30 min |
| Standard (L2) | 12–15 min | 12 min | 12 min | ~40 min |
| Extended (L3–L4) | 20–35 min | 15 min | 15 min | 50–60 min |

For 2-hour lectures, one extended case or two standard cases fit the activity block alongside the lecture's other segments (see the teaching blueprint's session structure). Written homework use: a case + the rubric makes a self-contained assignment with a two-page answer.

## 5. Difficulty progression map

```
        UNDERSTAND ──► DECOMPOSE ──► CRITERIA ──► INTERACTING DESIGN
        ────────────   ───────────   ──────────   ───────────────────
Level 1   locate the
          real problem,
          stakeholders,
          I/O/C
Level 2                 multi-step
                        reasoning,
                        quantify one
                        comparison,
                        name a trade-off
Level 3                               explicit
                                      weighted criteria,
                                      scored options,
                                      defend vs strongest
                                      counter-argument
Level 4                                             interacting
                                                    constraints,
                                                    ranked trade-offs,
                                                    name the
                                                    under-served,
                                                    defend by criteria
```

**Level placement rules** (used by curators and validators):

- A case is **L1** if the task is complete once the problem is correctly *characterized*.
- A case is **L2** if solving requires *multi-step reasoning* and at least one *quantified or structured comparison*.
- A case is **L3** if the answer must be chosen by an *explicit criteria set* and defended against a named counter-argument.
- A case is **L4** if *at least four constraints genuinely interact* (each affecting the others) and no option satisfies all of them — the student must show what they traded away.

**Reading the "Expected solution characteristics" honestly.** Several cases (PB-215, PB-306, PB-310, PB-314, PB-417, PB-419, PB-421) are constructed so that *multiple defensible answers exist*. In those, the characteristics describe the reasoning quality bar — not a verdict. Instructors should mark those cases "open-verdict" when assigning them.

## 6. Content protection

- The four level files are **student-facing** and published on the course site.
- Instructor-only material — full solution guides, alternative-solution analyses, common-mistake lists, marking notes — is kept **outside** the published site entirely (the same separation used by module guides, slide decks, and lab answer notes; ask your instructor for access arrangements).
- The bank validator (`scripts/validate_case_bank.py`, part of the course's check suite) enforces:
  - unique case IDs across the bank (no repeats),
  - correct level labels and ID-level agreement,
  - all required student-facing fields present and non-stub,
  - **no solution-guide content in public files** (structural leak scan),
  - internal links resolve (with the MkDocs strict build as the second net).

## 9. Assessment rubric (all levels)

| Criterion | Weight | What distinguishes full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Problem definition | 20% | The *real* problem — not the surface symptom; stakeholders complete, with the non-obvious ones found |
| Constraint awareness | 15% | All binding constraints named; *implicit* constraints surfaced (the L4 marker) |
| Decomposition & reasoning | 25% | Problem broken into tractable parts; reasoning steps explicit and ordered; assumptions labelled as assumptions |
| Option comparison & criteria | 20% | Alternatives genuinely compared; criteria explicit and, at L3+, weighted; quantification where possible |
| Trade-offs & risks | 10% | What the chosen path costs, and who pays; risks named with mitigations |
| Communication & honesty | 10% | Clear structure; uncertainty stated; claims calibrated to evidence ("open-verdict" cases not forced to a single answer) |

**Level adjustment.** The same rubric applies at all levels; the *bar* rises: an L1 answer earns full marks for clean problem characterization; an L4 answer is incomplete without the sacrificed-stakeholder paragraph.

## 10. References and sourcing rules

- Cases cite the course's own lecture pages and its published reference list (textbook, NIST/CISA/WCAG materials) — no fabricated citations.
- Every scenario involving an organization (clinic, hospital, city, platform, NGO) is **fictional and marked as such**; no real-world incident is represented as fact.
- Where a case touches a fast-moving factual area (AI capabilities, service terms, product pricing), the case supplies all needed facts internally and flags the area as one where *current* external facts must be checked, not assumed.
